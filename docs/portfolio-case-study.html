Case study · Internal tooling · 2026

LedgerLab.

Two hand-maintained spreadsheets tracked a PHP 1.33 billion cold-storage construction project. They had stopped being reportable. I rebuilt them as a single-user React + Express + MySQL ledger system — and found, on the way in, that the spreadsheet had been adding together three kinds of money that must never be summed.

1.0
The brief

The spreadsheet still worked. Reporting from it didn't.

The accountant had built both workbooks herself, and they were doing their job as a record. What had broken was everything downstream: answering "how much is left on item 4.0" meant reading across sheets that had drifted apart, and the weekly numbers could no longer be defended when someone asked where a figure came from.

The two .ods files held 20 named sheets between them, plus 12 dead file:///D:/… link sheets pointing at a payroll workbook on someone else's drive. One sheet stored 43 weekly payroll blocks as a 246-worker × 43-week pivot grid. Another laid four weekly summaries side by side across 66 columns.

Every PO number, supplier name and charge code is typed by hand. There is no ERP. The answer that reshaped the whole project

That single fact moved the centre of gravity. If nothing upstream validates the data, the app is not a reporting layer over a source of truth — the app is the source of truth, and its real value is entry quality, not charts.

2.0
The finding

Three ledgers, presented as one column of money

The summary sheet had six money columns that were routinely summed. They are not the same kind of number. Budget is an approval. Contract value is a commitment. Payments, replenishments, cash advances and payroll are disbursements. Adding across those boundaries produces a figure that describes nothing.

The consequence is a single word with two meanings. The spreadsheet showed one "remaining" column. There are two, and they are PHP 482.6 M apart:

₱ 369,348,846

Both are now first-class columns in v_budget_vs_actual, labelled in full in the UI. The old sheet only ever showed the first — which is the one that makes a project look further along than the bank balance does.

The corollary that cost the most to get right

PHP 26.6 M of customs duty, freight, terminal handling and marine insurance sat in the source looking like contract value. Accounting confirmed it is landed cost — company expense, not an increase to contract. Modelled the obvious way, budget items 4.0 and 5.0 would have read PHP 26.6 M more committed than they are. It counts as disbursement; it never touches contract_amount.

Migration proof

Reconciled to the centavo, or explained to the centavo

A migration is only believable if it reconciles against the source's own control totals. The first extraction pass left a PHP 28.3 M hole. After a round of questions to accounting and a rewritten parser, that closed — and the two residuals that remain are each traced to a specific row or a specific set of weeks.

Data quality

Bad rows are a feature of the product, not a cleanup task

The tempting move is to drop or auto-correct rows that won't parse. Both destroy evidence. Anything ambiguous loads with needs_review = 1 and surfaces in a queue where the accountant — the only person who knows the answer — fixes it in place.

Transactions dated 1900, 1958, 2005 and 2015 survived into the source unnoticed. Charge codes appeared as 3.8.7. and 3.2.5.2. with trailing dots. One supplier was recorded as Inaguration. Every one of those traces to a free-text field — which is what section 5.0 is about.

6.0
Engineering

Four decisions that keep the arithmetic honest

Money never becomes a float

At PHP 1.33 B, IEEE-754 doubles cannot represent centavos reliably. Amounts are DECIMAL(18,2), and mysql2 returns them as strings — which is left exactly as it is. Arithmetic happens in SQL, or in decimal.js when it genuinely must happen in JS. No peso amount is ever parseFloat-ed and added to another.

Aggregation lives in views, not in Node

Eleven views carry the reporting logic — v_budget_vs_actual, v_po_balance, v_planning_line_spend, v_weekly_burn, v_po_retention, v_vat_component and five per-ledger roll-ups. Rows are never pulled into the application to be summed, which keeps one definition of every total instead of one per endpoint.

Retention is not a payable

Eleven POs carry a holdback totalling PHP 25,319,221.48 — the largest a single PHP 12.78 M. That is money the supplier is not yet entitled to, so it gets its own line rather than being folded into an outstanding balance. It also has to survive a trap in the source: "100% Upon Completion" is a payment schedule, not a 100% retention.

Budgets are revised by append, never by update

Two items are over budget — fire protection by 61%, bollards by 19% — and both will be adjusted. Revisions are inserted into budget_revisions with before, after, effective date and reason. Overwriting the figure in place would have made every past variance unexplainable.

7.0
Scope

What I deliberately did not build

One user, one project, one local machine. The main risk to a tool like this is not technical difficulty — it is building the version that impresses instead of the version that ships.

  • ✕ Approval workflow The Coordinator → SVP → CEO chain happens outside the app. It's a monitoring tool.
  • ✕ Roles and permissions One user. The users table is a username and a bcrypt hash.
  • ✕ Multi-tenancy The schema supports a second project; the UI does not, until there is one.
  • ✕ Payroll calculation Daily rates and DTR detail no longer exist in the source. The app records weekly net pay and says so.
  • ✕ Foreign-currency features FX columns exist because rates vary per payment — but no PO is currently held in foreign currency. Dormant until needed.

Sixteen runtime dependencies across both halves of the stack, total. Rollout is deliberately unglamorous: the app and the spreadsheet run in parallel for two to four weekly cycles before the sheet is retired.

Screens

Overview (budget vs actual, KPIs, cost trend, weekly burn, alerts) · budget-item drill-down with WBS tree · purchase orders with balance, retention panel, payment terms and attachments · replenishment ledger · cash advances · additional payments · payroll by period and by worker · suppliers.

The extractor

A 672-line Python script, standard library only, no dependencies. It parses content.xml out of the .ods archives directly, because pandas.read_excel cannot open these files at all — they contain formula-error cells. It prints its own row counts, reconciliation and retention table on every run, so the migration re-proves itself rather than being trusted from memory.

Operations

Backup endpoint and a scheduled PowerShell dump, an append-only audit_log with created_by / updated_by on every write path, soft-delete with a restore modal, and CSV export and print layouts on every ledger — because the figures feed billing and someone will eventually ask who changed what.

9.0
Takeaway

The hard part was never the code

The build is a conventional CRUD dashboard, and deliberately so. What made it worth doing was the four weeks of questions before it: what kind of money is this column, why doesn't this total match, is this expense a contract increase or an expense, and what should happen to the row nobody can explain.

Every answer became a constraint written down where the next person will trip over it — a schema comment, a view, a picker instead of a text box, or a flagged row that refuses to be quietly averaged away.
